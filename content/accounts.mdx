# Accounts and Colour

## One rule, held absolutely

**Hue means which mailbox. It never means anything else.**

Not priority, not category, not read state. That restraint is what makes the
question answerable without reading: your eye can only use colour as an
identifier if colour is not simultaneously being spent on three other things.

The account rail down the left edge is a **filter**, not navigation. Picking an
account narrows what you are looking at; it does not take you somewhere else.

## Why eight, and why they look equal

The eight hues sit on a circle at a **fixed lightness and a fixed chroma** —
45° apart. Equal lightness is not an aesthetic choice: it is what makes them
comparable. A palette where one colour is brighter than another turns into a
ranking whether you meant one or not, and you would start reading the bright
one as "important".

The ring stops at eight for a measured reason. Ten equally spaced hues at fixed
lightness and chroma sit at or below the discriminability threshold under
deuteranopia. Eight that genuinely work, plus a neutral for anything past them,
is better than ten that half work.

<Callout type="info">
  **The monogram is not decoration.** Wherever an account hue appears, its
  two-letter monogram appears with it. Under any colour-vision deficiency the
  monogram is the primary channel and the hue is the redundancy — never the
  other way round.
</Callout>

## The chrome wears the account

Selecting an account tints the rail and the sidebar with its hue — the same
idea as a per-workspace theme in a chat app, and it stops in the same place:
**the message stream never takes the tint**. Long body text keeps a neutral
ground, and the coloured things inside a message — label chips, account dots —
keep something uncoloured to sit on.

Only the chroma moves. The lightness stays the base's own, so no account's
chrome is louder than another's and body contrast is identical across all eight.

## Labels keep their own colours

Your Gmail labels come through with the colours you gave them, rendered inline
on the message row.

That is deliberate, and it is the opposite call from the account hues: the
taxonomy is yours, and recolouring it to fit a palette would make your own mail
unfamiliar. A chip answers "where does this one belong"; the sidebar answers
"show me that place".
