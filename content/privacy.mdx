# Privacy

Two claims, and both are structural rather than promised. Before them, the four
disclosures a policy is supposed to make in plain words rather than leave to be
inferred from the design.

## What Google data Vicenda uses, and what happens to it

### What is accessed

Vicenda asks for one Google permission,
`https://www.googleapis.com/auth/gmail.modify`, and nothing else. No other
Google product is reached: Contacts, Drive, Calendar and the rest are neither
requested nor reachable.

Through it the app reads:

- **the address of the mailbox you connect**, which is what labels that account
  in the app;
- **the list of messages** in the mailbox, and each message's **headers** —
  sender, recipients, subject, date, and the labels it carries;
- **the body of a message**, when the app renders it in a channel or you open
  it;
- **the label list** of the mailbox, mirrored into the sidebar with its own
  colours;
- **the mailbox's change history**, which is how the app syncs after the first
  fill without re-reading everything.

### How it is used

To show you your own mail on your own Mac, and to change it when you ask. That
is the whole of it.

**Reading** builds what you see: the message list, the channels, the threads,
the cards drawn for machine mail, and the search index that lives in the same
local database.

**Writing** is three operations, each one you start:

- **mark read or unread** — removes or adds Gmail's `UNREAD` label;
- **archive** — removes Gmail's `INBOX` label;
- **move to trash** — Gmail's own trash, which you can undo from Gmail.

Vicenda never deletes a message permanently, never sends mail, and never
creates or modifies a draft on the server. The composer saves drafts locally
and cannot send.

The **optional local model** is no exception. It runs on your Mac, is shown one
message at a time, and produces a card in your own local database. Nothing
about a message is sent anywhere to build it, and no message is ever used to
train it or anything else.

None of this data is used for advertising, for profiling, for analytics, or for
training any model.

### Who it is shared with

**Nobody.** Vicenda runs no server, so there is no infrastructure of the
developer's for your mail to pass through, sit on, or be processed by. It is
not sold, not transferred, not disclosed, and not shared with any third party,
service provider or affiliate. The only network traffic carrying your mail is
between your Mac and Google.

Two **optional** settings, both **off unless you switch them on**, make an
outbound request a careful reader should know about, and neither carries
message content:

- **The sending domain's logo** asks the sender's own domain for its published
  brand record, falling back to that domain's favicon. What travels is the
  domain name, which the sender already knows.
- **Gravatar** sends `gravatar.com` a hash of a sender's address. The address
  itself does not travel, but the hash identifies it, so Automattic learns that
  this Mac corresponds with that person.

The third source, the **Mac's own address book**, is local and is the one that
is on by default; nothing leaves the computer for it.

### How it is protected

- **In transit**: every call to Google is over HTTPS, using the system's own
  TLS.
- **At rest**: mail is stored in a single SQLite database in your home
  directory, created `0600` inside a `0700` folder — readable by your macOS user
  account and by nothing else on the machine. Settings shows you its exact path.
- **The credential**: the OAuth refresh token is kept in the macOS login
  **Keychain**, one item per account under `com.gfazioli.Vicenda.oauth`, and is
  never synchronised to iCloud. It is not written into the database, into a
  preference, or into a log.
- **Nothing is copied off the machine**, so there is no backup of your mail
  under anybody else's control, and no account of ours that could be breached.

### Deleting it, and taking the permission back

Removing an account in Vicenda removes its mail from the local database, and
deleting that file removes everything at once. Neither touches your mailbox at
Google.

The permission is yours to withdraw at any time at
[myaccount.google.com/permissions](https://myaccount.google.com/permissions).
Revoking it stops all further access immediately; the app then has no way to
read or change anything.

### Limited Use

Vicenda's use of information received from Google APIs adheres to the
[Google API Services User Data Policy](https://developers.google.com/terms/api-services-user-data-policy),
including the Limited Use requirements.

## No server, ever

Vicenda runs no server. Not for sync, not for search, not for the clever parts.
Your mail is read from the provider straight to your Mac and stored in a local
database; nothing is relayed through anybody's infrastructure on the way.

That is not a policy that could be changed in an update without you noticing —
it is the reason the app is shaped as it is. There is no account to create, no
service to sign in to, and no telemetry, so there is nothing to switch off.

The [optional local model](/docs/local-model) is no exception: it runs on the
Mac, reads a message there, and the only thing it produces is a card in your
own local database. Nothing about the message is sent anywhere to build it.

## No pixel

Opening an email is the one everyday act that reports itself. A tracking pixel
is a one-pixel image on a sender's server: your client fetches it to draw the
message, and the fetch itself says *this person opened it, at this time, from
roughly here*.

Vicenda does not make the request. Before a message is rendered, a rule list is
compiled that blocks **every** remote resource, of every type, unconditionally.
Not a filter list to tune — the reading tier has no legitimate remote fetch, so
the correct rule is the total one and there is nothing to keep current.

<Callout type="warning">
  **It fails closed.** If that rule list cannot be compiled, Vicenda does not
  render the HTML anyway — it falls back to the message's text, which cannot
  fetch anything, and tells you why. A safety claim printed unconditionally but
  enforced conditionally is worse than no claim, because you stop checking.
</Callout>

JavaScript is off at the configuration level, not by a preference something
could relax. A link in a message opens in your browser rather than navigating
inside the reader, so nothing can replace the message with a page that then
looks like the message.

## Sender pictures

Vicenda can show a face beside a sender, and each source is a separate decision
because each has a different cost.

- **The Mac's address book** — local. The photo is one you already have and
  nothing leaves the computer. On by default.
- **The sending domain's logo** — fetched from the domain's own published brand
  record, falling back to its favicon. It identifies a *company*, not a person,
  and repeats across hundreds of messages. Off by default.
- **Gravatar** — off, and it should stay off unless you want it. Asking a third
  party about an address tells them that this Mac corresponds with that person.
  It is a smaller leak than a tracking pixel — once per address, and to
  Automattic rather than to the sender — but it is the same kind.

## Demo Mode

Demo Mode masks every identity at the point where the app reads its own data:
names, addresses, monograms, labels and timestamps. It is there so the app can
be shown, screenshotted or demonstrated without showing the mailbox.

![The Unread view as it appears in Demo Mode: every sender name, address and subject on screen is invented at the render layer](/screenshot-unread.png)

That image is Demo Mode, and so is every other screenshot on this site. There is
no before-and-after here on purpose: the "before" would be somebody's real mail.

It **fails closed**: anything it cannot positively identify as a vendor gets
masked, rather than the other way round. And it masks the *pictures* too — a
masked name over a real brand's logo is still that brand, and looks handled
while leaking exactly what it claims to hide.
